Interlude 6
-----------

To the Archive, from your Chronicler lately of the Capitol, presently some thousands of miles removed from any post I hold a formal commission to occupy —

I do not have the folio I would have liked to send with this. That document, three years in the keeping, burned along with the sail room that held it, in circumstances I hope to have occasion to explain to you in person rather than on paper. What follows instead is reconstructed from memory, and from a fortnight of very frank conversation aboard a ship I do not command, with a crew whose full particulars I am still assembling notes on. I have made it a habit, these many years, never to send you an incomplete picture. I am breaking that habit now, because incomplete is better than silent, and because some of what I have learned will not keep until I can deliver it whole.

--

Begin with the plainest matter, since it is the safest to commit to paper. The war you and I both grew up hearing about is not being fought. It has stalled, not ended — the Shatters threw up a barrier between the fleets that neither side has found a way around, and both nations have spent what they might have spent on each other on their own recovery instead. Do not mistake this for peace. Nothing has been negotiated; the two sides have simply been unable to reach one another, and the Kingdom, for its part, has used the lull to look west rather than to stand down, pouring what it can spare into a crossing meant to carry it around the Maelstrom entirely. I am told, though I have not verified it myself, that the Southern Houses read the New World the same way — as the next arena for the old rivalry, now that the old one has gone quiet. What I can attest to more confidently is the shape of the gap itself: a ship carries news across the Maelstrom in weeks at best, and what survives the crossing is whatever a captain or a trading house has chosen to repeat. I have spent three years assuming my own information was current and finding out, this last fortnight, how much of it was not. I offer that as caution to the Archive as much as to myself: whatever any of us believe we know of the other side of that water right now, treat it as provisional.

There is a harder matter I must set down more carefully, because it touches the young people I have fallen in with, and because I do not yet trust my own conclusion enough to want it repeated before I can stand behind it in person. They were declared heretics of the Church some years past, over a matter I have reason to believe I understand better than most — I will not detail it here, save to say it concerns an object, and a doctrine, and is not itself in question. What troubles me is the manner of the declaration: a public bounty, issued with a speed and a certainty that is not how the Church ordinarily moves against a handful of unproven youths. My own old notes, written before I understood any of this, already recorded alarm at the Church's sudden interest in them — I did not know, at the time, that I was watching the opening move of something already decided. I have a name in mind for the hand that decided it. I have turned it over enough now that I am no longer troubled by how well it fits, only by how much I would prefer to be wrong. I will not commit that name to this or any other page. If this folio reaches you before I do, ask me nothing of it in writing; I will lay out the whole of my reasoning across a table, where it belongs.

--

You will recall my old interest in the "Advance" and in Barrows, her captain — a man I have described to you before, in other correspondence, as one of a small and careful fellowship whose name I do not commit to paper, and whose members I do not name in writing even to you. I can now confirm, from these young people's own report, that the "Advance" was tracked two years past to an island the Islanders and the Elves both call Birdcrown, and has not been seen or heard from since. She has not been struck from any Navy list I am aware of, nor formally reported lost, which I find as strange as anything else in this account — a missing warship, on a mission that mattered to people who do not forget such things easily, going unremarked in the official record is either an oversight or a silence someone has chosen to keep. I do not know which. I will say plainly that I am uneasy about it in a way I cannot yet justify with evidence, only with pattern: this is the second man of that fellowship to go quiet on that same stretch of water, on business the fellowship itself sanctioned, and I do not like patterns I cannot explain.

On the matter of the Shatters themselves and what caused them, I can add rather more than I expected to when I left the Capitol. The light that is said to rise from a Totem in the instant of its destruction is not, I am now confident, a detail specific to what happened at Spider Island — it matches, closely enough that I no longer think it coincidence, an account already forty years old and independently recorded. My own circle's working belief is that a Totem's power does not simply vanish when the pillar breaks; it is loosed all at once, ungoverned, which is why the aftermath scales with whatever the Totem itself was worth destroying. I will add one thing further, and ask that you treat it exactly as carefully as I intend it: there are more such sites, known to people I trust, than the two the world already has cause to mourn. I do not know how many. I do not know where. I offer it only so the Archive does not mistake two incidents for the whole of the pattern.

--

A smaller, tidier matter, since you have always had patience for questions of Church structure. I have had cause recently to confirm, from a captured vessel's own crew, the fates of two priests aboard her — a senior woman, Margot, trusted with a relic and formidable in her own right, and a junior man, Arnim, neither talented nor especially unlucky, simply posted where the Church needed a warm body willing to go. Both are missing, presumed drowned, and I do not expect that to change. What is worth recording for the Archive's own reference is the question that was put to me afterward: whether either of them held some rank above the ordinary — a "summoner," in the word used to me, as though the Church kept some office for commanding power beyond a cleric's reach. It does not. I can say this with the flat certainty of a man who has spent a decade cataloguing that hierarchy: Twin Eminences, Archons, High Clerics and Canons, Vicars, Clerics, Curates, Prelates, Deacons, Acolytes, and one office outside that ladder entirely, the Censure — sent when the Church expects to find and destroy something it has already judged heretical, and equipped accordingly. Neither Margot nor Arnim was a Censure. If the Archive ever needs the distinction restated plainly: a difficult priest is a nuisance; a Censure is a different order of danger altogether, and I hope none of you ever need the difference explained to you by better evidence than this letter.

I owe you, too, an accounting of my own conduct, since it explains why this folio is so much later than it ought to be. I went looking for a ship called the Fortnum in person rather than by correspondence, once I learned she had been reassigned from ordinary convoy work to something closer to hunting — the particulars of what, I will not set down here, save that it touches the same doctrine as the matter above. That was my own choice, not an assignment, and I do not regret making it. I did learn, aboard her, rather more than a Chronicler's ordinary remit allows, and rather less discreetly than I intended — enough that her captain, a man named Mason, had every reasonable cause to treat me as something closer to a spy than a guest. I will not pretend otherwise to you, of all people. He did not treat it as a hanging matter. He confined me, took my writings into his own keeping for safety rather than destroying them, and asked only my word that I would not attempt escape while he held them — a bargain he kept in full, and one I broke only once the writings themselves were no longer his to hold, at which point I told him so to his face and considered the matter closed between us. He accepted that without argument, which I have not stopped thinking about since. He was, by every measure I could take of him in the time I had, an honourable man doing an ugly job competently, and I do not enjoy that his death sits at the end of my own rescue.

--

The last matter I set down here I still do not entirely understand, and I ask the Archive's patience with an account that ends in a shrug rather than a conclusion. Chroniclers are taught to call and steady message birds over long distances; a very few of us are taught that the same faculty, cautiously and rarely used, extends further, toward creatures larger and less biddable than any bird — not command, properly understood, so much as an urgent suggestion laid across an animal's own instincts, which it is free to answer or ignore as it chooses. I had used this, in small and careful measure, more than once during my confinement aboard the Fortnum, to harry her with lesser creatures and buy myself what openings I could. In the last extremity of my escape from her, with the outcome very much in doubt, I called again, with rather more need behind it than I had ever risked before, and something answered that was larger than anything I had thought myself capable of reaching — large enough to break the ship in two. I do not know why that attempt succeeded where my earlier, smaller ones only startled a watch. My best guess, and I offer it as no more than that, is that the size of the need shapes the size of the answer, but I have no way to test it, and no wish to test it again if I can help it. Nor can I explain a stranger detail from earlier in that same voyage: on one occasion, a lesser summoning of mine was answered not only by a serpent but by a burst of mortar fire from a source no one aboard, on either side of the fighting, ever claimed. I have turned over every ordinary explanation I can think of and settled on none of them. I raise it here only because a loose thread left unrecorded is a thread the Archive cannot pull on later, should it ever need to.

I began this letter meaning to apologise for its lateness and its gaps, and I find, having written it, that I am less troubled by either than I expected to be. My folio on these young people was, before it burned, the most densely annotated document in my keeping, and still the least finished — I have watched them long enough now to trust that it will stay that way for some time yet. I mark this page in the corner as I always do. Those of you who know why will know.

— R.
